\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Accuracy and retained state}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} relates the accuracy gains to retained storage: the proposed correction reduces mean MSE by 16.99\% using 4528 bytes, whereas full ELF reduces it by 19.68\% using 301984 bytes (median array counts). This is a 2.69 percentage-point difference in mean reduction at approximately 1/66.7 of the retained storage. The detailed rows report absolute errors for the legacy, refit, and additional-site conditions. The proposed correction reduces both MSE and MAE relative to the uncorrected base in all 72 legacy/refit conditions and all four additional-site conditions before seed aggregation. Across the 72 main conditions, the mean relative MSE reduction is 16.99\%. The improvements therefore extend beyond a single architecture or base-training variant within this evaluation.

The proposed method is not the most accurate comparator overall. The full ELF configuration achieves a mean MSE reduction of 19.68\% relative to the same static forecasts. It has lower MSE than the proposed method in 54 of the 72 main conditions and all four additional-site conditions. Table~\ref{tab:main_results} shows that the proposed method is competitive on ETTh1 and ETTh2 with DLinear, and on Appliances with DLinear. ELF is stronger on several other combinations, including both ETTm datasets. The outcome depends on the dataset and base architecture.

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy_storage} and Table~\ref{tab:storage_results} place these errors alongside retained array sizes. The proposed method uses a median of 4528 bytes. Full ELF uses 301984 bytes after conversion to independent real coordinates and packed symmetric statistics. This is approximately 66.7 times the proposed count. The representation change reduces full ELF storage from 946504 bytes while preserving its forecasts to numerical precision. The observed gap should therefore be assessed using the revised storage count.

Among the smaller Fourier configurations, \texttt{in5} and \texttt{in9} retain 5472 and 11200 bytes and yield mean MSE reductions of 9.59\% and 14.54\%, respectively. The proposed method provides a larger mean reduction with fewer retained bytes than these two configurations. In contrast, \texttt{compact5} retains only 2784 bytes and yields a 9.42\% reduction. These comparisons identify an accuracy--storage tradeoff. They do not establish a universal best configuration across all resource budgets.

Table~\ref{tab:learned_results} compares the proposed correction with the learned adapters on their corresponding selected joint base models. For MAE warmup, the mean MSE reduction relative to the globally blended adapter is 19.27\%, 18.98\%, and 16.69\% at widths 2, 4, and 64. With MAE plus SF warmup, these reductions are 20.15\%, 19.54\%, and 17.21\%. The corresponding MAE reductions range from 8.82\% to 11.29\% across the six configurations.

For every width and warmup condition, the proposed correction has lower MSE and lower MAE in all 12 dataset--backbone pairs after averaging losses over seeds. Thus, the MSE and MAE win counts are both 12/12 for each row of Table~\ref{tab:learned_results}. These are paired comparisons within shared datasets, not 72 independent datasets or a test of statistical significance.

The small adapters themselves also improve on their paired base-only training runs. At widths 2 and 4, their raw forecasts reduce mean MSE by 2.22--3.68\% and MAE by 1.27--1.98\%. The comparison therefore includes learned correction models that provide measurable benefits under the training protocol. This check evaluates the joint-training procedure as a whole. The same-base comparisons in the first two numerical columns of Table~\ref{tab:learned_results} isolate the subsequent choice of correction method.

Accuracy and storage again favor different configurations. The median retained sizes of the globally blended adapters are 1584, 1968, and 13488 bytes at widths 2, 4, and 64. The proposed method uses 4528 bytes. Its accuracy advantage over widths 2 and 4 comes with a larger retained state, whereas the width-64 comparison favors it on both reported quantities. The gains over learned adapters cannot be assigned entirely to the endpoint feature because the regression architecture and estimation procedure also differ. Appendix~\ref{app:learned} provides seed-wise and dataset--backbone comparisons for width 64.

\subsection{Contribution of endpoint selection}
Table~\ref{tab:endpoint_ablation} holds the regression structure and retained array size fixed while changing the scalar residual summary. On the SF joint base models, retaining the endpoint reduces MSE by 5.43\%, 5.49\%, and 6.94\% relative to retaining the first residual, middle residual, and block mean, respectively. The corresponding MAE reductions are 3.38\%, 3.34\%, and 4.14\%.

The MAE-only warmup condition gives the same direction of improvement. Its MSE reductions range from 4.70\% to 6.00\%, and its MAE reductions range from 2.93\% to 3.61\%. Each summary comparison favors the endpoint in all 12 seed-averaged dataset--backbone pairs for both metrics. The direction also holds for each of the three individual seeds. The raw, unblended correction retains positive mean improvements over all three summaries under both warmup conditions, so the observed contribution is not solely an effect of the global controller.

These results support the choice of the endpoint within the tested compressed regression. The first and middle controls have the same number of inputs and coefficients, which rules out an increase in regression size as the explanation for their difference from the endpoint variant. The mean control also has the same stored size, but its role depends on the output component: it duplicates the residual DC input for $j=0$ and supplies the DC input to the non-DC regressions for $j>0$. Its result cannot therefore be attributed generally to redundant-feature regularization. The comparison tests the endpoint against this particular allocation of the retained DC information. None of these comparisons proves that the endpoint is optimal among all possible residual summaries.

\subsection{Complete input construction versus endpoint alone}
Table~\ref{tab:input_ablation} compares the complete construction with simpler inputs on the 72 main conditions. Relative to endpoint-only regression, the complete method reduces mean MSE by 4.71\% and MAE by 2.26\%, with lower MSE in all 72 conditions. The raw comparison also favors the complete method in all 72 conditions, with a mean MSE reduction of 4.08\%. Thus, the benefit over endpoint alone persists without blending. It requires more retained arrays: 4528 rather than 2288 median bytes.

Removing current forecast coefficients increases MSE in all 72 main conditions; the complete method's mean paired reduction is 2.45\%. Removing residual DCT coefficients gives a mean paired reduction of 2.17\%, but favors the complete method in only 67/72 conditions. The five exceptions are ETTh2 with DLinear (legacy seeds 0 and 2, and all refit seeds), where the complete method's MSE is 0.01--0.22\% higher. The compressed residual history therefore contributes on average but is not necessary for improvement in every condition.

The complete method also improves on endpoint-only regression on the separately trained width-64 bases: mean MSE reductions are 6.53\% and 6.64\% for MAE and SF warmup, respectively, with wins in all 12 seed-averaged pairs for each. Against the control without residual DCT inputs, the corresponding win counts are 11/12; ETTh2 with DLinear remains the exception. All four additional-site conditions favor the complete method over endpoint alone, with a mean MSE reduction of 4.04\%. These comparisons support average complementarity of the inputs within this regression family, rather than universal superiority of every input.

\subsection{Setting and candidate-selection sensitivity}
Increasing $K$ from 1 to 8 improves the main mean MSE reduction from 13.46\% to 17.69\% while increasing retained arrays from 1576 to 8464 bytes (Table~\ref{tab:s_sensitivity}). The reference $K=4$ gives 16.99\% at 4528 bytes. It is therefore an accuracy--storage choice, not the accuracy-optimal setting in this pool. With $K=4$ fixed, the tested one-factor changes to ridge, half-life, or controller window yield mean MSE reductions of 15.91--17.00\%. This supports persistence of the average benefit across these settings, without establishing insensitivity to their interactions.

Table~\ref{tab:temporal_selection} reports the later-half evaluation. On the main conditions, the fixed reference reduces MSE by 18.80\% relative to the static base, compared with 19.52\% for prefix selection among 13 settings and 19.26\% among all 21 candidates. Their paired reductions relative to the fixed reference are 0.82\% and 0.53\%, respectively. The prefix-selected losses exceed the corresponding suffix-oracle losses by 0.47\% and 0.77\% on average. These quantities are finite-pool diagnostic gaps, not estimates of all selection bias incurred during method development.

The 13-setting prefix procedure selects $K=8$ in 49/72 main streams. The 21-candidate procedure selects it in 42 streams, while also selecting the middle summary in five, the first summary in one, and the control without residual DCT inputs in three. Selection minimizes MSE across candidates with different storage requirements. Neither its gains nor its implementation cost can be attributed to the reference method's 4528 bytes. Positive average reductions also persist in all three expanding-prefix intervals (Table~\ref{tab:s_origins}), and the separately reported joint-base groups retain substantial gains. All these results remain retrospective analyses of series used during development.

\subsection{Condition-wise behavior and failure cases}
Figure~\ref{fig:condition_performance} separates legacy, refit, and additional-site results. Improvements over the static base occur alongside losses to stronger comparators. In particular, ELF has lower MSE and MAE on both additional sites for both architectures. The proposed method also has a small negative result against coefficient-only regression: on Fox with PatchTST, its MSE is approximately 0.113\% higher. This control retains fewer bytes, so endpoint augmentation does not provide an improvement for every evaluated base forecast.

Full-period improvements can also coexist with local deterioration. Figure~\ref{fig:worst_interval} shows the interval selected by the maximum local relative MSE criterion in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}. It occurs on ETTm1 with the legacy PatchTST model and seed 0. Across blocks 124--155, the proposed method has 19.63\% higher MSE than the static base. Over the complete evaluation stream for that condition, it reduces MSE by 11.17\%. The local excess and the full-period reduction use different time intervals in their denominators.

The selected interval demonstrates a limit of adaptation from completed residual blocks. The controller estimates correction strength from past errors, and favorable past performance does not ensure improvement in the next interval. The trace establishes the occurrence of local harm. It does not identify a unique causal mechanism for that episode. We therefore retain both the full-period metrics and the adverse interval when interpreting effectiveness.
